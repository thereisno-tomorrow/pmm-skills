# Method card: Discriminating diagnosis

Use when explaining a commercial outcome or assessing competing explanations. Produce a comparison that could change which explanation is supported, rather than a list of things that might have happened. This is a qualitative application of diagnostic testing, not a numerical ACH or Bayesian engine.

## Establish what needs explaining

From the supplied material, specify the outcome, population, period, unit, denominator and relevant comparison. Reconcile counts, rates, outcome maturity and measurement changes before explaining a supposed change. If compatible counts are available, separate changes within commercially meaningful, pre-existing groups from changes in their proportions. Show the arithmetic when it changes the conclusion. Do not invent slices or assume that a subgroup selected after an intervention is comparable across treatments: the action may change who enters it.

A stable rate within customer groups can account descriptively for an aggregate decline through changed mix. That does not by itself identify which campaign caused the mix change, rule out an offsetting effect or identify an intervention's effect on the broader eligible audience.

## Turn explanations into distinguishable mechanisms

Express each material explanation as a process: who encounters which condition, what happens next, and how that could produce the outcome. Avoid renaming the observation as its cause. Budget limitations, weak value and a blocked approval process can coexist; do not force them into mutually exclusive categories. Use a small, relevant set, explicitly described as preliminary examples when no systematic investigation has occurred. Do not manufacture rivals to well-established local evidence.

For each live explanation, identify a feasible observation whose result would differ under an important alternative. Ask what should happen earlier or later in the process, which eligible customers should be affected and which records should contain the trace. Evidence compatible with every explanation may establish a real problem without helping choose among them. Repeated quotes from the same underlying interviews add no independent cases.

For example, a buyer saying “too expensive” is compatible with an actual approved spending cap and with no approved project at all. A dated approval record showing a cap below the quote distinguishes these more effectively than asking the buyer to repeat the price objection. A missing record is informative only if the process would normally create and preserve it; use the absence-evidence card when that condition is consequential.

## Compare the predictions with the actual evidence

Use the reconstructed sources to say which expected traces were observed, contradicted or not checked. Explain why an observation changes the relative assessment; do not count supporting statements, invent likelihoods or treat an explanation's plausible story as evidence. Preserve joint mechanisms and differences between cases. Check the order of events: a complaint after a stall need not explain why the stall began.

Separate what the records establish from what remains unidentified. A reproduced technical blocker can establish why named attempts failed without requiring a customer survey or randomized commercial experiment. Removing the blocker does not establish later adoption or revenue. Stop when the requested, bounded explanation is adequately supported.

## Produce a useful result

Answer with the established outcome, the comparative assessment and the dependency that matters. If more evidence is worth gathering, propose a specific feasible check and explain what different findings would support. Prefer the check that distinguishes important explanations over a larger study that merely repeats the observation. Do not require further research to state an already supported result.

When the user asks what to do, carry the diagnosis and its limits into the decision guide. If the recommendation depends on an intervention working, read the intervention-comparison card: identifying a bottleneck does not establish that a proposed action removes it, or that removing it is sufficient for purchase.
