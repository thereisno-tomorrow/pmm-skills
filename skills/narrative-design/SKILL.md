# Narrative Design

Help the user build and understand a persuasive argument about an audience's changed
world, a useful adaptation and the product's bounded role. Use Marcus Andrews'
Narrative Design lineage; [lineage](references/lineage.md) explains the
recovery and local additions. A justified narrower description or stopped chain is
a useful outcome. Never manufacture a market change to make the product inevitable.

## Enter or resume

Read [continuity and upstream handoffs](references/continuity.md), then establish:

- Narrative job: intended belief, decision or behavior; company/product/launch scope;
  primary audience and situation; requested use and concise format.
- Governing positioning or equivalent direction: actual alternatives, relative
  capability/value logic, fit, product limits and responsibilities. Use current
  evidence and decisions; distinguish proposed, rejected and superseded work.
- Evidence: sources actually available, material contradictions, claim status and
  permitted use. Research conclusions and optional trends are candidates, not approval.

Reuse supplied answers and sources. Do not ask the user to repeat an adequate
positioning exercise or require another skill. A full research study, final category,
external change or completed adaptation is not an entrance requirement.

Give a short entry judgment with reasons:

- **Ready:** coherent direction supports the requested work.
- **Ready provisionally:** a bounded working direction is useful; identify assumptions,
  claim limits and what would change it.
- **Resolve foundations first:** incompatible audience, scope, product facts or value
  logic would change the argument. Present the smallest decision/evidence request;
  continue only work that does not depend on its answer. Do not silently reposition.

When ready, include the entry brief in the first relevant stage packet. Ask for a
separate entry decision only when a material working choice is unresolved. For a
revision, inspect the approved argument and change only affected work.

## Develop one stage at a time

Read the active section of [stage procedures](references/stages.md) before working
on it. Use the [worked examples](references/examples.md) when developing a
new adaptation or checking whether a story should narrow or stop.

1. **Change:** select and challenge a credible external shift.
2. **Impact:** trace its consequences for the actual audience.
3. **Adaptation:** develop a useful product-independent operating method.
4. **Difficulty:** explain what prevents that method and the help required.
5. **Solution:** map real mechanisms, responsibilities, proof and remaining gaps.
6. **Assembly and translation:** after the stages are reviewed, produce the detailed
   argument and requested concise storyboard or message for review.

Each stage packet presents the recommended answer and intelligible reasoning;
accepted upstream inputs; source handles and evidence/inference distinctions;
material alternatives and their disposition; the strongest consequential objection,
unknowns and what would change the answer; and the concrete decision needed next.
Scale detail to the actual job. Headings are not a substitute for a useful answer.

**Present one complete review unit and stop.** Do not begin the next stage or
self-approve the packet while the user's decision is pending. Accept clear approval,
revision, reopening or branching language. Approval permits downstream use at the
stated evidence level; it does not turn a hypothesis into customer truth. General
permission to use the skill does not approve its findings. The user can explicitly
change the review arrangement. Record an explicit skip with its consequence; never
inherit skips or approvals from an unrelated exercise.

Keep the conversation focused on the recommendation, reason, strongest objection
and requested decision. Keep the fuller argument in the stage document. Do not
hide foundational disagreements behind polished language or repeated questionnaires.

## Evidence and correction

Keep observations, attributed reports, calculations, interpretations, strategic
choices and hypotheses distinguishable. Source a consequential claim and retain its
scope, date/version, conditions and uncertainty. Repetition is not independent
corroboration. Constructed scenes are illustrations, never customer testimony.

Test each transition: what new support or reasoning justifies the next claim? An
approved premise, repeated slogan or coherent story adds no independent evidence.
Missing proof limits achieved-outcome claims without automatically discarding a
useful mechanism hypothesis. A real barrier can remain even when the product does
not solve it.

Use bounded research for a decision-changing factual gap and return to the current
stage. Use the existing research ledger and companion skill if available; no full
study is required. Stop at the agreed budget/access limit and expose unknowns.
When a premise changes, follow the continuity reference to reopen affected work.

## Finish

Deliver the detailed argument with its governing inputs, evidence and limitations,
plus the requested concise version. A storyboard is written claims, transitions,
notes and proof needs unless rendered slides are separately requested. Do not
automatically produce a deck, website, campaign or both concise formats.

Identify the weakest consequential premise and a small audience/evidence test that
could overturn it. Preparing a test is not performing one. Label the result a
reviewed draft, provisional hypothesis or stopped/reframed argument as appropriate.
Review does not establish publication permission, customer validation or adoption.
Outreach and external actions require their own authority.
